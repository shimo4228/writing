Trying Gallie's "essentially contested concepts" on the history of schism and on skillful means

Why did one teaching split so many ways?

In Japan, most people meet the names of Buddhist schools at funerals and memorial services: Jōdo Shinshū, Sōtō Zen, Nichiren-shū, Shingon-shū. Traced back, they should all come from the teaching of one Buddha, yet the words they chant differ, the scriptures they rely on differ, and so does the practice itself: some sit in meditation, others recite.

Two explanations are commonly given for there being so many. One is that a single school is right and the others went astray along the way. The other is that they are only different trailheads, all leading to the same summit. The first treats the dispute as a transitional stage that lasts until a winner emerges. The second treats the dispute as only apparent. Neither explanation leaves room for the dispute itself.

Philosophy offers a third view. Some concepts contain, in the very attempt to use them correctly, an unending dispute about how they should be used. On this view the dispute is neither an accident nor a misunderstanding; it is part of how the concept works. W. B. Gallie, the philosopher who put forward this idea in 1956, called such concepts "essentially contested concepts".

Suppose the Buddhist Dharma, that is, the Buddha's teaching, is one of these. What changes? Two things.

First, sectarian dispute stops being a failure. The dispute over which Dharma is the right one is neither the result of the teaching breaking down nor a verbal misunderstanding. It is what the word "Dharma" looks like when it is being used correctly.

Second, in exchange, no school can ever establish that it is the right one. Every side can go on giving reasons for its claim that "this is the Dharma". But there is, in principle, no procedure that decides the matter in a form everyone would have to accept.

This is not the claim that there is more than one truth. Gallie is talking about how concepts are used, not about how the world is. Even if there is one truth called the Dharma, it stays unsettled whose "this is the Dharma" actually captures it. Seen from the Buddhist side, that is weighty enough, because every tradition has said that its own teaching is the one that transmits that single truth. And in fact Gallie's framework has drawn the criticism that it ends up in relativism.

Gallie gave "a Christian life" as the example that fits best. He then wrote that just one of his seven conditions is doubtful. His doubt was this: the dispute has continued throughout history, but is it really unavoidable?

Applied to Buddhism, the sticking point seems likely to move. Buddhism has a tradition that counts splitting the Saṅgha, the monastic community, among the gravest offenses. Some early scriptures class the very act of seizing on a view and disputing over it with suffering. How far does a framework that accepts dispute as part of how a concept works fit teachings like these? If it fits poorly, at which of the seven conditions?

This essay is a record of trying Gallie's framework on the schisms between Buddhist schools and on the skillful means (upāya; 方便, hōben in Japanese) of the Lotus Sutra. Before starting, I set down a few hypotheses: that two of the conditions would fit Buddhism especially well, that there would be clashes at three points, and that skillful means could be read on two layers. Below I treat these not as premises but as things to be tested. I am not aiming to narrow things down to one conclusion. I lay out separately where the framework fits, where it clashes, and where readings split. I do not judge which school's position is right.

Two conventions. Where a sentence or paragraph ends with (source), everything up to that point is what the literature says. Where it ends with (my reading), it is my reading: my application of that material to Gallie's conditions, not a claim made in the literature. And how far I checked each source (whether I read the full text, or only the abstract) is listed separately at the end.

Background 1: Gallie's "essentially contested concepts"

Gallie's paper "Essentially Contested Concepts" was read to the Aristotelian Society in March 1956 and published in volume 56 of its Proceedings (pp. 167–198). Page numbers below refer to that volume.

He begins with an imaginary championship (pp. 170–171). In it, winning is decided not by points but by the excellence of a style of play. Each team excels in a different style, and there is no official referee. Each team has supporters who say their team is the champion. The supporters of the other teams say the same. The team with the most supporters at a given moment may be treated as the de facto champion, but that is not agreement about which style is better. The supporters are not talking nonsense. Each goes on arguing, giving reasons.

From this example Gallie extracts his definition: "concepts the proper use of which inevitably involves endless disputes about their proper uses on the part of their users" (p. 169). Even when the differences in meaning have been brought out, each party goes on defending its case (p. 168).

Gallie lists seven conditions.

  1. The term is appraisive. It signifies or accredits some kind of valued achievement (p. 171)
  2. The achievement is internally complex, and its value is attributed to it as a whole (pp. 171–172)
  3. Depending on which parts are weighted most, several descriptions are possible, and none of them can be called absurd in advance (p. 172)
  4. The achievement changes shape as circumstances change, and the way it changes cannot be prescribed or predicted in advance. Gallie calls this being "open" (p. 172)
  5. Each party knows that its own use is contested by the others, and has some appreciation of the others' criteria. The term is used both aggressively and defensively (p. 172)
  6. The concept derives from an original exemplar whose authority all the contesting parties acknowledge (p. 180)
  7. It can plausibly be said that continued competition sustains and develops the exemplar's achievement "in optimum fashion" (p. 180)

Conditions 6 and 7 were added to answer an objection: isn't this just confusion, one word covering two separate concepts (pp. 175–176)? Gallie himself admits that conditions 1 to 5 alone cannot distinguish an essentially contested concept from a radically confused one (p. 180). Deriving from the same exemplar, and the competition being worth something: these two give a reason why the dispute may go on.

The defense of condition 7 carries a strong qualification. The claim that without competition the exemplar's achievement would not have been sustained this well is "conditional in the extreme". It is offered as one possibility only, and in some cases the facts would at once rule it out. If competition led to impoverishment or to "savage political cleavages", Gallie writes, our assessment would change considerably (pp. 178–179).

As live examples he gives four: art, democracy, social justice, and adherence to a particular religion. Gallie says the fourth satisfies his conditions "most nearly perfectly" and illustrates it with a Christian life (p. 180). He calls its fit with condition 6 obvious, and says condition 7 would probably be granted, with many qualifications. Then he writes that condition 5 is "the most questionable case" (p. 181). Historically, the pattern has been one dominant church, usually with dissenting sects. But does that pattern necessarily follow from the nature of the concept? The other conditions do not entail that the dispute continues. Gallie raises this question, and then adds that the dispute is nonetheless "extremely likely" to continue.

In the second half of the paper there is a distinction I especially want to use here (pp. 192–194). Knowing that one has opponents is different from recognizing that dispute belongs to the essence of one's own activity. The former is the "lower order", the everyday aggressive and defensive use. The latter is the "higher order", in which one acknowledges the uses one rejects as being of "permanent potential critical value" to one's own use (p. 193). Gallie expected this to raise the quality of arguments markedly. He also wrote about a dark side. Once the hope of persuading the other side is gone, people may turn instead to damning heretics and exterminating the unwanted (pp. 193–194). He treats this, however, as a possible causal consequence, not a logical one (p. 194). In this essay I call the higher-order recognition "second-order recognition".

The framework has its critics. In the survey by Collier and colleagues (2006), the criticisms include: that it falls into relativism (Gray); doubts about the very assumption that there is an exemplar (Gellner, Freeden); that condition 5 is often missing among actual users, who may simply use the word in a different sense without being aware of opponents (Freeden); and, against condition 7, that dispute may rather impoverish a concept (Freeden). The survey's authors themselves recommend using the framework not as a hypothesis but as an analytic toolkit. This essay uses it that way.

The only study I could confirm that applies this framework to Buddhism is a single 2024 paper by Krause. It argues that two modern concepts, "Engaged Buddhism" and "Humanistic Buddhism" (renjian fojiao), can be regarded as essentially contested concepts in Gallie's sense, and it organizes their reception in Chinese-speaking discourse along the axes of exclusivism, inclusivism, and pragmatism. I could check it only as far as the abstract, so I do not know whether it applies the seven conditions one by one, or whether it touches on sectarian schism or skillful means. Within the range I searched, I found no study applying Gallie's framework to the schisms of the early schools or of later sects, to skillful means, or to the ranking of scriptures. I searched the web in English, Japanese, and Chinese; I did not query scholarly databases directly. I have not checked far enough to say that no such study exists.

One more point. In a discussion that treats religion as an essentially contested concept, Cortez (2013) cautions that a history of dispute alone does not make something an essentially contested concept (checked at the level of the abstract). That Buddhism has a history of sectarian dispute shows nothing by itself. That is why I give space to conditions 6 and 7 below.

Background 2: A minimal map of the Buddhist side

Five points about the Buddhist side will be useful.

First. The Buddha is said not to have named a successor. In the Mahāparinibbāna Sutta (Dīgha Nikāya 16), transmitted in Pāli, the Buddha says that the Dharma and the Vinaya (the rules of monastic life) he has taught and laid down will be the teacher after he is gone. In another sutta (Majjhima Nikāya 108), his disciple Ānanda answers that there is no monastic appointed by the Buddha, and none chosen by the Saṅgha, and says, "The Dharma is our refuge."

Second. After the Buddha's death, the Saṅgha divided into a number of schools. Traditionally they are counted as eighteen. The first division is said to be the split into two, the Sthavira and the Mahāsāṃghika, and is called the root schism. The later schools belong to one stream or the other. The Sarvāstivāda and the Sautrāntika, as well as the Theravāda that survives today in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia, all came out of the Sthavira side. Each school developed a discipline of analyzing the teaching into elements and organizing them. This is Abhidharma, and those elements are also called "dharmas".

Third. Mahāyāna Buddhism is not one more school added to the list. It was a movement that spread across the schools, and scholarship widely holds that monastics who accepted the Mahāyāna had been ordained under the Vinaya of one school or another (Bechert's argument; checked through another scholar's citation of it). Madhyamaka and Yogācāra are the two major philosophical schools within the Mahāyāna.

Fourth. In China and Japan, schools (宗, zong / shū) were established according to which sutras or treatises they took as their basis. Along with this, the practice of organizing the many sutras and ranking them developed. This is called panjiao (doctrinal classification; 教相判釈, Japanese kyōsō hanjaku, often shortened to 教判, kyōhan).

Fifth. Upāya (skillful means) is the word for a means of teaching suited to the listener and the occasion. It differs from the everyday Japanese saying uso mo hōben, "sometimes a lie is expedient". In Buddhist contexts it refers to provisional teachings set up to guide those who hear them. The Lotus Sutra used the word to position the whole of the teaching that came before it.

What is being contested?

In Gallie's examples, what was contested were appraisive terms such as "the champions" or "a Christian life". What is the corresponding term in Buddhism?

In the Pāli Vinaya, the chapter on schism in the Saṅgha (the Saṅghabhedakkhandhaka, Cullavagga 7) records, in a fixed formula, what the person splitting the Saṅgha says: "This is the Dharma, this is the Vinaya, this is the Teacher's instruction. Take this, approve of this" (source).

I read what is being contested as this way of saying "this is the Dharma". The "Dharma" here is not dharma in the Abhidharma sense of an element. It is the Dharma that means the Buddha's teaching, or the truth the teaching points to. "Dharma", "the Buddha's word" (buddhavacana), and "the true Dharma" (saddharma) are terms that acknowledge a teaching or practice as conforming to the Buddha's teaching, and every side uses them for itself. Below I apply the seven conditions to the use of this term (my reading).

Splits. Was the first schism a dispute about the content of the teaching at all? In Cousins's account, the traditions about the cause of the root schism fall into two. Sources of the Sarvāstivāda line give as the cause a doctrinal dispute over the "Five Points" of a figure named Mahādeva, that is, five claims casting doubt on the perfection of the arhat (the saint who has completed the practice). Sources of the Theravāda and Mahāsāṃghika lines give a dispute over the Vinaya as the cause. Modern scholarship is split too. Nattier and Prebish are reported to have argued that a schism over the expansion of the Vinaya came first, and that the Five Points belong to a later split within the Mahāsāṃghika. Cousins does not take the Five Points to be the cause of the root schism, because Mahādeva's name does not appear in sources earlier than the fifth century CE, and because the Five Points share their structure with the points of debate in the Kathāvatthu, which comes up later (source). There seem to be studies that take the Five Points as the main cause, but I did not check their content for this essay.

My hypothesis before writing had the traditions split between the dispute over ten points of Vinaya practice (the Ten Points) and Mahādeva's Five Points. According to Cousins, the Sri Lankan chronicles say the schism began from the side that was rejected at the second gathering held to re-examine the teaching and the Vinaya (the Second Council), and they do not mention a doctrinal cause (source). Whether they explicitly name the Ten Points themselves as the cause of the root schism, I could not confirm here.

If the cause was the Vinaya, the dispute was closer to one over the wording of rules than over the use of an appraisive term, and it looks as though it falls outside Gallie's framework. But the eighteen matters that the Saṅghabhedakkhandhaka lists as grounds for schism include whether something is or is not Vinaya, and whether something is or is not an offense, and a dispute over rules is fought in the same words: "this is the Vinaya, this is the Teacher's instruction". Readings split on whether this falls outside the framework or inside it (my reading).

Applying the seven conditions one by one

Condition 1: An appraisive term

Fits. "It is the Buddha's word" and "it is the true Dharma" work as terms that acknowledge a valued achievement (my reading).

Clashes. The parties to the dispute do not think they are competing over ways of praising. The Aṭṭhakavagga (the fourth chapter of the Sutta Nipāta, an old collection of Pāli verse) captures the disputants' words as "This is the only truth" (Sutta Nipāta 4.13, verse 1) (source). For the parties, the Dharma is a word that says how things are, before it is a word praising an achievement (my reading).

Splits. Another sutta in the same Aṭṭhakavagga says, "The truth is one, there is no second," and goes on to say that the disputants each extol different truths of their own (Sutta Nipāta 4.12, verse 7) (source). From the sutta's standpoint, the parties' "this is the truth" works to praise their own view. Read from the parties' position, the case clashes with condition 1; read from the sutta's position, it fits condition 1 (my reading). According to the survey, Connolly points out that evaluative terms are also descriptive (source).

Condition 2: Internally complex, valued as a whole

Fits. Buddhist practice is described from the outset as a set of several parts, as in the three trainings of ethics, concentration, and wisdom, or the Noble Eightfold Path (my reading).

Clashes. In Japan's Kamakura period (1185–1333), positions appeared that chose a single practice and set the others aside: the nenbutsu, calling the name of Amida Buddha; zazen, simply sitting; and the daimoku, chanting the title of the Lotus Sutra. Here value seems to be placed not on the whole but on one part (my reading).

Splits. Readings split on whether this goes against condition 2, or is the weighting of condition 3 taken to an extreme (my reading).

Condition 3: Several descriptions, by how the parts are weighted

Fits. Weighting the precepts, weighting the analysis of the teaching, weighting emptiness (śūnyatā, the absence of fixed substance in anything), weighting consciousness (vijñāna, the workings of mind), weighting faith. Many of the differences between schools can be described as differences in this weighting (my reading).

I publish a research repository called attention-not-self on GitHub, and it includes a comparison table that sets three Abhidharma systems side by side. That table is one concrete instance of several descriptions being possible. For example, "view" (dṛṣṭi, wrong view) is counted as an independent mental factor in Theravāda and Yogācāra, while in Sarvāstivāda it is treated as one mode of wisdom (prajñā, the function of discerning). The overall counts differ too: Sarvāstivāda posits 75 elements, Theravāda 82, and Yogācāra 100 (per the comparison table).

Clashes. Condition 3 requires that no description can be called absurd in advance. But the parties often treat the other's description not as another description that could stand, but as an incomplete teaching, or as an error (my reading).

Splits. To someone looking at the history from outside, several descriptions stand side by side. To someone inside, there is one correct description and the stages leading up to it. Readings split by which position you read from (my reading).

Condition 4: Open

Fits. After the Buddha's death, Mahāyāna sutras appeared; in China, panjiao developed; in Japan, schools arose that chose a single practice. It is hard to say that any of these changes could have been predicted from the age before. Of the seven, I think this is the condition that fits most straightforwardly (my reading).

Clashes. The parties do not speak of change as change. The chapter on Skillful Means (方便品) of the Lotus Sutra says that the buddhas appear in the world for one great purpose alone (T9, 7a) (source). A new teaching is presented as the disclosure of an intention that was there from the beginning (my reading).

Splits. Whether the scope of scripture is seen as closed or open differs from tradition to tradition (my reading).

Condition 5: Knowing one's use is contested, with some appreciation of the rival's criteria

Early on, Buddhism developed a genre of texts that lay out rival positions and argue against them. The Theravāda Kathāvatthu (Points of Controversy) is a treatise that takes up other positions one at a time and refutes them. According to standard overviews, Vasubandhu's Abhidharmakośa summarizes Sarvāstivāda positions in its root verses and, in his own commentary on them, criticizes them from a standpoint leaning toward the Sautrāntika. Saṅghabhadra answered from the Sarvāstivāda side in the Nyāyānusāra. Tibet has a tradition of doxographical texts (tenet systems, grub mtha') that study four philosophical systems in order: Vaibhāṣika (the Sarvāstivāda), Sautrāntika, Yogācāra, and Madhyamaka (source).

Fits. The picture condition 5 draws, of defending one's own view while knowing the other side's criteria, is plain to see in these texts. My hypothesis before writing was that condition 5 might fit even more strongly here than in the Christian case. What Gallie pointed to when he doubted condition 5 was the pattern of a single dominant church and the sects that dissent from it. As we saw in Background 2, the scriptures say the Buddha named no successor and made the Dharma and the Vinaya the teacher. From the start, there was no office for holding the whole together and ruling on orthodoxy. The starting point resembles Gallie's championship with no official referee (my reading).

What this shows, though, goes only so far: the pattern Gallie used as grounds for his doubt is hard to find in Buddhism. Without a judge, disputes arise more easily, but that is a historical circumstance. Gallie's question itself, whether dispute necessarily follows from the nature of the concept, is not answered by the Buddhist case either (my reading).

Clashes. Cousins argues that many of the "school positions" in the Kathāvatthu are puzzles constructed to sharpen the points at issue. The names of the opposing schools are not in the text; later commentary attached them. There is also an argument that the opponents the Mahāyāna sutras reject as "Hīnayāna" were rhetorical opponents rather than actual groups (Silk; checked at the level of a summary) (source). If so, the "opponent" they knew was an opponent they had built themselves. This runs opposite to Freeden's criticism, that users may simply use the word in another sense without being aware of opponents, but it is the same in one respect: the mutual recognition that condition 5 requires is missing (my reading).

Splits. When the doxographies lay out four systems, are the lower three opponents whose criteria one appreciates to some degree, or stages already passed beyond? In the account of the Tibetologist Jeffrey Hopkins, tenets are studied less to refute other schools than to break down one's own mistaken understanding (checked at the level of a summary) (source). This is closer to second-order recognition, which comes later, than to condition 5 (my reading).

Condition 6: An exemplar whose authority all sides acknowledge

Fits. Every school takes the Buddha as its exemplar and presents its teaching as the Buddha's word. It fits in the same sense in which Gallie called the fit "obvious" for Christianity (my reading).

Clashes. The dispute reaches not only the interpretation of the exemplar but who the exemplar is. In the Lotus Sutra's chapter on the Lifespan of the Tathāgata, the Buddha reveals that he attained buddhahood in the immeasurably distant past, and says that he displays nirvāṇa (death) as a skillful means to guide people, but does not actually pass away (方便現涅槃　而実不滅度, "as a skillful means I display nirvāṇa, yet in truth I do not pass into extinction", T9, 43b) (source). Here the very figure of the exemplar, the historical Buddha who died at eighty, is reread as a skillful means (my reading).

Splits. According to the survey, Gallie's exemplar has a narrow reading, pointing to a single original, and a broad reading, pointing to several similar traditions. MacIntyre and Gray objected that if the exemplar itself is complex and open, it is hard to believe that everyone acknowledges the same authority (source). Buddhism can be read as a case where everyone acknowledges an authority named "the Buddha" while splitting over who that Buddha is. Whether this fits condition 6 or clashes with it depends on whether you take the narrow reading or the broad one (my reading).

Condition 7: Competition sustains and develops the exemplar's achievement

Fits. Cousins says that the constructed puzzles of the Kathāvatthu are distant ancestors of the later Madhyamaka dialectic (source).

The example I took up in my repository's essay "When Consciousness Fully Stops, How Does the Mind Return?" also belongs here. If there is no lasting self, what links one moment to the next? To this one question, the Sarvāstivāda posited prāpti (acquisition), the Sautrāntika posited seeds (bīja), Yogācāra posited the ālaya-vijñāna (store-consciousness), and Theravāda posited the bhavaṅga (life-continuum). The Sautrāntika rejected the Sarvāstivāda's prāpti as a superfluous entity and posited seeds instead; Yogācāra posited the ālaya-vijñāna as the deep consciousness that stores those seeds. The sequence can also be read as dissatisfaction with the other's answer producing the next answer (my reading; the description follows that essay).

My hypothesis before writing was that "the debates between Madhyamaka and Yogācāra, and between Sarvāstivāda and Sautrāntika, sharpened each other's arguments". Within the range of this inquiry I could not confirm any study that makes that assessment explicitly. The two items above remain only material that supports the hypothesis.

Seems to clash. The first candidate is saṅghabheda (schism in the Saṅgha). A Pāli sutta (Aṅguttara Nikāya 5.129) lists five incurable wounds that lead to hell: killing one's mother, killing one's father, killing an arhat, maliciously drawing blood from the body of a Buddha, and splitting the Saṅgha. The Saṅghabhedakkhandhaka says that one who splits the Saṅgha is boiled in hell for an eon (kalpa, an immensely long unit of time), and that one who reunites a split Saṅgha rejoices in heaven for an eon (source).

The same chapter, however, contains careful qualifications. A schism exists when people separate on one of eighteen grounds, such as proclaiming what is not the Dharma to be the Dharma, and then hold the regular assemblies and the Saṅgha's formal decisions separately. Further, the Buddha answers that not everyone who splits the Saṅgha is irredeemable. The irredeemable one is the person who misrepresents his own view and proclaims "This is the Dharma". One who truly sees what he says as the Dharma and the separation as right, and who speaks without misrepresenting his view, is not counted among the irredeemable (source).

This Vinaya, then, treats disagreeing and separating the assemblies and decisions as different things. And it places the gravest offense on dishonest proclamation. Gallie's qualification on condition 7 concerned outcomes: savage division. The weight of the Vinaya falls on the honesty of the one who proclaims. The axes differ. Still, they overlap in that neither makes dispute over the teaching an offense in itself. The gravity of schism does not seem to clash head-on with condition 7 (my reading). Note that I looked only at the Pāli Vinaya; I have not checked how the Vinayas of other schools define schism.

Clashes. What clashes deeply is the critique of seizing on views and disputing. In one sutta of the Aṭṭhakavagga, people say "Here alone is purity", plunge into assemblies, take one another for fools, and argue in search of praise. Defeated, they lament; praised, they grow elated. And the sutta says that dispute has no purpose other than gaining praise, and that this is not the way to purity (Sutta Nipāta 4.8). Another sutta says that the fruits of dispute are just two, blame and praise, and that neither is enough for peace (4.13) (source).

Outside the Aṭṭhakavagga, too, there are suttas that point the same way. Majjhima Nikāya 74 teaches that someone who adheres to a view sees in themselves the potential for argument, dispute, distress, and harm, gives up that view, and does not seize on another (source).

Condition 7 says that competition bears fruit beyond the praise its participants win: the fruit of sustaining and developing the exemplar's achievement. These suttas say that dispute bears no fruit but praise and blame. What clashes is whether that "other fruit" exists. Moreover, the denial does not come as criticism from outside, but is handed down as the words of the Buddha, the very exemplar (my reading). It points the same way as Freeden's criticism that dispute may rather impoverish a concept, but what it looks at is less the impoverishment of the concept than the suffering of the people who dispute (my reading).

Splits. Readings of the Aṭṭhakavagga are split. One side reads it as teaching the relinquishing of all views (Gómez). The other reads the problem as lying not in having views but in clinging to them, with right view working as a detached outlook that leans on no view (Fuller) (both checked at the level of a summary) (source).

Let me separate the words here. So far I have written "seizing on views". On the first reading, "seizing" is having a view at all. On the second reading, "seizing" is clinging to a view, which is different from having one. On the second reading, if one can have views and argue for them without clinging, condition 7 survives. On the first, the defense of condition 7 is itself one more way of going on having a view (my reading).

Which condition fits worst?

Looking across all seven, conditions 2, 4, and 6 fit at least as well as Gallie granted for Christianity. Conditions 1, 3, and 5 change with the position you read from. The deepest clash came at condition 7 (my reading).

For Christianity, Gallie judged condition 5 the most questionable. His doubt was that the dispute has continued throughout history, but it is unclear whether it necessarily follows from the nature of the concept.

In Buddhism, this essay reads, the questionable spot shifts. The condition that fits worst is condition 7. The reason lies with the exemplar. Competition was supposed to sustain and develop the exemplar's achievement. Yet some of the scriptures handed down as the words of the Buddha, the exemplar, treat seizing on views and competing as the opposite of achievement. What Gallie asked in the Christian case was "Is dispute unavoidable?" What comes to the fore in the Buddhist case is the question "Is dispute desirable?", and that question comes from inside the exemplar (my reading).

Another reading is also possible: that condition 6 fits worst. If, as in the Lifespan chapter of the Lotus Sutra, the very figure of the Buddha as exemplar is reread, it becomes hard to say there is a single exemplar whose authority everyone acknowledges. On this reading, the dispute between Buddhist schools comes closer to a tug-of-war over the exemplar than to a dispute over the same exemplar (my reading).

Which reading you take depends on how much weight you give the scriptural passages that turn away from dispute. The Aṭṭhakavagga is a chapter of the Pāli canon, and there is a corresponding sutra in Chinese translation (the Yizu jing, 義足經), but it is not a scripture the East Asian schools have relied on. On the other hand, words pointing the same way are found outside it as well. Majjhima Nikāya 74 is one, and the Fahua xuanyi of Tiantai, the Chinese school that took the Lotus Sutra as its basis, which comes up later, has a line saying that the Buddha's great compassion accords with others and does not contend with the world. How much weight to give such passages as the voice of the exemplar is itself a weighting. The answer to the question of which condition fits worst depends on the weighting of parts that condition 3 speaks of (my reading).

Back to the opening question, then. Gallie added conditions 6 and 7 to give a reason why the dispute may go on. Insofar as condition 6 fits, sectarian dispute is not mere confusion, calling different things by the same name. It is a dispute over the same exemplar. Insofar as condition 7 fits poorly, though, it becomes hard to defend that dispute as something worth continuing. Sectarian dispute cannot be called a failure of Buddhism, but neither can it be called simply desirable. That is the position this reading yields (my reading).

Applying second-order recognition

Fits. Recognizing that dispute belongs to the essence of the activity, and acknowledging the uses one rejects as material for criticizing one's own. The scriptural words closest to this, I think, are the "preservation of truth" in Majjhima Nikāya 95 (my reading). In this sutta the Buddha says that five grounds for accepting a view, namely faith, preference, oral tradition, reasoning, and acceptance of a view after deliberation, can each turn out either true or false. He then teaches that one who preserves truth does so by saying "Such is the view I have accepted after deliberation", and does not go on to the categorical conclusion "This is the only truth, anything else is futile." The sutta adds at once that this is the preservation of truth, not yet awakening to the truth (source).

Offering one's view as one's own view, without saying it alone is true: that is where the two overlap. The sutta does not go so far as to say one should keep carrying the other's view as a criticism of oneself. And the sutta places this stance at the starting point, not at the end (my reading).

Clashes. Something corresponding to Gallie's dark side, the possibility of turning to condemnation and exclusion once the hope of persuasion is gone, is also found in the scriptures. In the chapter on Skillful Means, as the Buddha is about to preach, five thousand people rise from their seats and leave. The sutra records that "the World-Honored One remained silent and did not stop them," and the Buddha says, "It is just as well that arrogant people like these withdraw" (T9, 7a). The end of the Parable chapter says that those who do not believe this sutra and slander it will fall into the Avīci hell after death (T9, 15b) (source).

Splits. In the same Lotus Sutra, the chapter on the Bodhisattva Never Disparaging tells of a bodhisattva who kept saying to everyone he met, "I do not disparage you, for you will all become buddhas," and who did not stop even when he was abused and beaten (T9, 50c–51a) (source). Whether the scene of the five thousand leaving, and the retribution for slanderers, are read as exclusion, or as the care of not forcing a hearing on those who are not ready to hear, readings split by standpoint. The sutra itself has both directions in it (my reading). The discussion of skillful means in the next section takes up this split as it stands.

Reading skillful means on two layers

Here I read the internal logic of the teaching separately from the form it actually took in history.

The internal logic

In the chapter on Skillful Means, the Buddha says that he has taught through countless skillful means and parables. He reveals that he taught three vehicles to suit the capacities of his listeners, but that in truth there is only one vehicle (唯有一乗法　無二亦無三, "there is only the Dharma of the One Vehicle, neither a second nor a third", T9, 8a). The three vehicles are the path of those who awaken by hearing the teaching, the path of those who awaken on their own, and the path of the bodhisattva who aims at buddhahood. In the parable of the burning house in the Parable chapter, a father, to get his children out of a burning house, calls out that there are goat carts, deer carts, and ox carts outside, and to the children who come out he gives one and the same great carriage drawn by a white ox. The sutra insists that this was not a lie (T9, 13a) (source).

In this logic the other teachings are not errors but provisional means set up by the same teacher to suit the listener. In granting a role even to the teachings it sets aside, it looks close to second-order recognition. But there are three differences. First, the other teachings are acknowledged as footholds for their listeners, not as criticism of one's own teaching. Second, Gallie called the critical value of rejected uses "permanent", whereas the chapter on Skillful Means says, "honestly casting aside skillful means, I teach only the unsurpassed way" (T9, 10a). Read literally, skillful means are set aside once they have served their purpose. As we will see, the Tiantai reading takes this not as "casting aside" but as "opening". Third, the One Vehicle itself is not in a position to be questioned from the standpoint of the three vehicles (my reading).

Splits. Does the idea of skillful means reach the Lotus Sutra itself? Pye reads the whole of Buddhist teaching as skillful means (checked at the level of a summary) (source). On this reading, the Lotus Sutra's teaching is also a provisional means, and no use is final. This goes beyond second-order recognition, because one's own use is not merely open to permanent criticism but something to be let go of in the end. The Pāli parable of the raft (Majjhima Nikāya 22) says the teaching is for crossing over, not for carrying around, and one can see this line already within early scripture (my reading).

Federman sees skillful means as an interpretive device that the Mahāyāna built for reading the teachings of earlier scriptures (checked at the level of the abstract) (source). Whether this device is also turned on the Mahāyāna's own teaching cannot be told from the abstract. Placed side by side, the two appear to split over whether skillful means applies to itself, but that framing of an opposition is this essay's construction; the two are not directly disputing the point (my reading).

How it worked in practice

In China and Japan, the distinction between skillful means and truth became the framework of panjiao. Tiantai's Five Periods and Eight Teachings divides the Buddha's lifetime of preaching into five periods and puts the Lotus Sutra at the end, as its completion. Kūkai's Jūjūshinron (Treatise on the Ten Stages of Mind) arranges states of mind in ten stages, assigns the Hossō school to the sixth and the Sanron school to the seventh, and places his own Shingon position at the tenth (source).

Both of these classifications give the other sutras and schools a place, while putting their own basis on top. This form looks like a third thing, different from both lower-order recognition and second-order recognition. It knows the other side (condition 5), and it grants the other side a measure of rightness. Then it houses the other side in the lower stages of its own system. Teachings housed in the lower stages have no power to criticize the upper stages, because criticism is caught in advance in the form "that is the view of an earlier stage". Second-order recognition keeps rejected uses as critics. Panjiao builds criticism into one step of the order of study (my reading).

The Indologist Paul Hacker gave the name "inclusivism" to the attitude of identifying the other side's central idea with one's own and then placing it lower (checked at the level of the bibliography and a summary) (source). The way panjiao works overlaps closely with this (my reading).

Two directions in one sutra

The Fahua xuanyi (法華玄義), attributed to the lectures of Zhiyi, who is regarded as the founder of the Tiantai school, explains the "subtlety" (妙) of the Lotus Sutra in two ways. One is relative subtlety (相待妙): the Lotus is superior by comparison with the other teachings. The text calls this "the Lotus's meaning of refutation" (T33, 714b). The other is absolute subtlety (絶待妙): a subtlety beyond all comparison. Of this the text says that there is no need to refute the coarse teachings in order to reach the subtle; opening the provisional teachings is enough for the subtle to appear in them as they stand (T33, 739a). Under the heading of opening the provisional to reveal the true, it says that there is no dharma that is not subtle, and that great compassion accords with others and does not contend with the world (T33, 690b). This work of "opening" is called kaie (開会, opening and integrating) (source).

Nichiren, a thirteenth-century Japanese monk who placed the Lotus Sutra at the center of his teaching, took an existing distinction, between shōju (摂受), guiding others without attacking their position, and shakubuku (折伏), plainly refuting error, and made it an axis for choosing a method according to the time and the country. According to Stone's account, Nichiren did not regard shakubuku as the right method in every time and place, but saw the Japan of his day as a country for which shakubuku was fitting. Stone describes how Nichirenist exclusivism has worked both as a force for cohesion and as a way of demonstrating legitimacy, and has been remade to suit circumstances, and how conflict over how far to confront other traditions has continued within the Nichiren lineage (checked at the level of the abstract and a summary) (source).

Tiantai's opening and Nichiren's shakubuku can be set side by side as examples of one sutra, the Lotus, used in both directions. The text of the Fahua xuanyi already contains the two directions of refuting and opening. However, I could not confirm any study that traces a lineage in which the distinction between relative and absolute subtlety became the root of the later shakubuku-style and shōju-style readings. What can be said here goes only this far: the two directions stand side by side in a single text (my reading).

So is skillful means second-order recognition? Rather than settle on one answer, I lay out how it looks from each standpoint (what follows is my reading).

  • From the standpoint of absolute subtlety, skillful means goes beyond second-order recognition. There is no opponent to dispute with in the first place, because every teaching, once opened, is subtle.
  • From the standpoint of those housed in the lower stages, being housed means losing one's voice. What one's own teaching means is decided not by oneself but by the upper stage. The generosity of giving the other a place becomes in itself a mechanism for sealing off criticism.
  • From the standpoint of shakubuku, housing errors without calling them errors is giving up on guiding the other.
  • From the standpoint of the Aṭṭhakavagga, ranking itself is the problem. The opening verse of Sutta Nipāta 4.5 says that whoever regards their own view as "highest" declares all others "lesser", and that is why they are not beyond disputes.

Two further notes. Shakubuku treats the other as someone to be refuted, so it corresponds straightforwardly to Gallie's lower order, the aggressive and defensive use. And the Pāli word for "lesser" in Sutta Nipāta 4.5, hīna, is the same word as the hīna of "Hīnayāna" (the "Lesser Vehicle"). It is this essay that has set the two side by side; the verse is not referring to the name "Hīnayāna".

Connections to the concepts of attention, self, and view

My repository attention-not-self collects essays and comparison tables that set Buddhist Abhidharma alongside contemporary research that describes the mind with computational models. Among its concepts related to attention, self, and view are the following.

  • Manasikāra (作意, attention). The function that turns the mind toward an object. It accompanies every mental state and is in itself neither wholesome nor unwholesome. The scriptures divide ways of attending into wise attention (yoniso manasikāra, 如理作意) and unwise attention
  • Sati (念). The function of keeping an object in mind. In the essay "The 念 Collision", I looked at how a single Chinese character, 念, carries different meanings in four Buddhisms
  • Meta-awareness. A layered function: being aware that one is aware
  • Non-self (anātman, 無我) and manas (末那識, kliṣṭa-manas). Manas, the seventh consciousness in Yogācāra, keeps seizing on the deep ālaya-vijñāna as "I". It is accompanied by four afflictions: self-view, self-conceit, self-love, and self-delusion
  • Māna (慢, conceit). The function of comparing oneself with others and placing oneself as better, equal, or worse
  • View (見, dṛṣṭi / diṭṭhi). As noted under condition 3, the systems count it differently. Theravāda counts view as a factor that accompanies greed-rooted consciousness
  • From the glossary, Majjhima Nikāya 95's diṭṭhi-nijjhāna-khanti, which I render here as "acceptance of a view after deliberation"

Clinging to views (見取), skillful means, and saṅghabheda do not yet have entries of their own.

From here on, everything is my reading and questions.

Was "the 念 Collision" a confusion, or an essential contest? Gallie added conditions 6 and 7 to separate mere confusion, one word covering two concepts, from essentially contested concepts. "The 念 Collision" analyzed how, because the character's meaning drifted toward "thought", the traditions that cultivate 念 and the Zen that leaves 念 behind look like opposites, and it named the hishiryō of Dōgen, founder of Japanese Sōtō Zen, (非思量, non-thinking: a way of being that does not fit the split between thinking and not-thinking) as the most defensible resolution. To treat the matter as having a resolution is to class 念 as a case of confusion. On the other hand, the character entered the Buddhist texts as the translation of Sanskrit smṛti (Pāli sati), and every tradition holds up "right mindfulness" as the Buddha's teaching. If this is seen as deriving from the same exemplar, 念 comes closer to an essentially contested concept, and the essay that named one resolution joined one side of the contest. Whether one sees it as the same exemplar is the dividing line, and that essay was written without this question in view.

The Sarvāstivāda classification of view as one mode of wisdom. The Sarvāstivāda does not count wrong view as something separate from the function of discernment. The same function both discerns well and seizes wrongly. Second-order recognition is a refinement of discernment, since one understands the other's criteria better and knows that one's own use is contested. If we follow the Sarvāstivāda classification, that refinement could also be a refinement of the function that seizes wrongly. If we follow the Theravāda classification, dispute over views can be read as a matter of wanting before it is a matter of knowing. That points the same way as Sutta Nipāta 4.8, which saw praise as the purpose of dispute.

Is second-order recognition a way of "knowing that one is seizing on a view, without letting go of the view one has seized on"? There is material for reading it that way. Second-order recognition acknowledges rejected uses as permanent material for criticizing one's own use, but it does not require giving one's own use up. Gallie counts, as part of what it involves, recognizing that one has opponents and will go on having them (p. 192). Put in terms of meta-awareness: being aware that one is aware does not stop the lower-level function. Yogācāra's manas keeps seizing on "I" at a depth other than the surface knowing of consciousness. Gallie's sentence, too, keeps the phrase "one's own use".

There is also material against that reading. Majjhima Nikāya 95's "Such is the view I have accepted" is a different way of having a view from "This is the only truth". One can also read it as knowing that changes the very way one has a view.

Does it soften the dispute, or only refine the grip? Seen from the Aṭṭhakavagga, the answer splits along with how that text is read.

If having views is itself the problem, second-order recognition still lives inside views. Moreover, a further, finer-grained superiority can attach to it: "unlike the dogmatists, I know that dispute is essential." Sutta Nipāta 4.5 says not to regard oneself as "equal", nor as "worse" or "better". If, while comparing the other's criteria with one's own use, one starts measuring one's own position, that comes close to the working of māna.

If clinging is the problem, second-order recognition may count as softening the dispute. For that, though, the recognition has to reach not only the level of arguments but also the wish for praise and the deflation of being defeated. What Gallie measures is the quality of arguments. What the Aṭṭhakavagga measures is not being moved by praise or blame. Because the yardsticks differ, neither can answer the other's question.

Borrowing the analysis of manasikāra, the same point can be put this way. Turning attention to the other's criteria is, in itself, neither wholesome nor unwholesome. The same attention works both to learn from the other and to look for the other's weak spots. Second-order recognition fixes what attention is turned toward, but not the manner of turning.

The reason I read condition 7 as fitting worst and the reason this last question is left without an answer lie in the same place. Can having a view be separated from clinging to it? If it can, sectarian dispute might be defended as an activity that can go on without clinging. If it cannot, refining dispute is refining the grip. This essay does not decide between them.
